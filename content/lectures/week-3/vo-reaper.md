## Choosing a microphone

The studio default for spoken voice is a large-diaphragm cardioid condenser. It gives a big, detailed sound that flatters most voices. If you have to record in an untreated or noisy room, a good dynamic microphone used close to the mouth rejects the room and isolates the voice. Keep that option in mind for PSA interviews recorded outside the studio.

## Placement

Placement decides more about your sound than the microphone does.

- For an intimate, up-close tone, put the mic 4 to 6 inches from the mouth with a pop filter in place.
- For a natural, transparent tone, back off to 9 to 12 inches or more.
- Set the mic at or slightly above eye level and angle it down at the mouth. This keeps the capsule out of the direct path of plosives while the read stays bright, and it encourages you to project forward.
- If you read from a music stand, remember that the stand is a hard reflector. It bounces your voice back into the mic out of phase and produces a hollow comb-filtered sound. Cover the stand with something soft, or angle it so reflections miss the mic.
- Put a piece of gaff tape on the floor where your toes go. Take 5 will then match take 1.

## Pop filter and proximity effect

Use a pop filter whenever you speak within 2 feet of the mic. Corbett's spacing rule is easy to remember: leave a fist's width between your mouth and the filter, and another fist between the filter and the capsule. Both gaps are needed for the filter to break up the plosive air blast.

Directional mics boost low frequencies as you get closer. This proximity effect peaks around 100 to 200 Hz. It sounds impressively big when the voice plays alone, but it turns to mud once music and effects join the mix. Back off a few inches before you reach for EQ.

## Levels in REAPER

Aim to peak around -12 dBFS and leave the top of the meter alone. This is the same headroom logic as the [recording lesson](/lectures/week-3/reaper-recording/). On a long read, ride the gain by hand: nudge quiet, swallowed words up and pull the level down before the loud lines. A gain ride during tracking sounds cleaner than heavy compression applied afterward.
